\begin{abstract}
Entangled enhancement is a defective retraining of a machine learning (ML) model that improves the updated model but fails to improve the whole system. Although the problem is known in the literature, little has been studied in real ML pipelines. This study empirically investigates real entangled enhancements in an autonomous driving pipeline consisting of perception, prediction and planning modules. We train an ML pipeline of three models on the nuScenes data set. We then investigate the change in the prediction module when the perception model is updated with a new data set. We test 30 different updates, which differ in the amount of new data, the number of training epochs, the random seed, and the initial perception model. Entangled enhancement is observed in the prediction module in 23 of the 30 updates. To analyze the cause, we conduct a comparative study, in which the prediction model is trained either on the ground truth or on the output of the perception model. An increase larger than the variation caused by a different random seed appears in 11 updates after training on the output of the perception model. No such increase appears after training on the ground truth. This result indicates that the adaptation of the prediction model to the errors of the perception model causes entangled enhancement.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
autonomous driving, entangled enhancement, machine learning system, model maintenance, retraining
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================================

A Machine Learning System (MLS) is often built as a pipeline of ML models sequentially connected, where each model uses the output of the preceding model. A model earlier in the sequence is upstream, and a model later in the sequence is downstream. Retraining an upstream model can improve that model and still fail to improve the whole system. Wang and Machida call this defecting update as \emph{entangled enhancement}~\cite{wang2024compsac,wang2025issre}.

Several studies describe the risk of entangled enhancement. Sculley et al.~\cite{sculley2015hidden} and Wu et al.~\cite{wu2021fixesthatfail} show that a change to one model of an MLS can make the whole system worse. Xu et al.~\cite{xu2024realworld} show that the prediction error increases when the input of the prediction model comes from a real perception model instead of the ground truth, which consists of the objects labelled by hand. However, none of these studies examined the significance of entangled enhancement in a real autonomous driving pipeline, consisting of perception, prediction, and planning modules.

In this study, we empirically investigate whether an update that improves the perception model increases the error of the prediction and planning models in an autonomous driving pipeline. When increased errors are observed, we further investigate the impact of adaptation of the downstream models. Adaptation means that the prediction and planning models, trained on the output of the perception model, learn to work with the errors of the perception model.

We train an ML pipeline on the nuScenes data set. We then update the perception model 30 times with a new data set. The 30 updates are not repetitions of one procedure. They differ in the amount of new data, the number of training epochs, the random seed, and the initial perception model. To analyze the impact of adaptation, we compare prediction and planning models trained on the ground truth with prediction and planning models trained on the output of the perception model.

We observe that the prediction error increases in 23 of the 30 updates after training on the output of the perception model. As a result, we confirm that entangled enhancement occurs in the prediction model. After training on the ground truth, the prediction error increases in only 5 of the 30 updates. None of these 5 increases is larger than the variation caused by a different random seed. A prediction model trained on the ground truth cannot adapt to the errors of the perception model. The increase after training on the output of the perception model therefore comes from adaptation.

\section{Related Work}
% ============================================================================

Studies on the maintenance of ML systems describe how a change to one model affects the whole system. Sculley et al.~\cite{sculley2015hidden} describe how a change to one model of an MLS changes the behaviour of every model that uses the changed output. Wu et al.~\cite{wu2021fixesthatfail} show that improving one model of a system can make the whole system worse. Wang and Machida~\cite{wang2024compsac,wang2025issre} model when an MLS should retrain each of its models, using the probability that a retraining causes entangled enhancement. In this study, we examine the adaptation of the downstream models as a cause of entangled enhancement. The downstream models adapt during training to the errors of the upstream model. The retraining then changes those errors, which disturbs the downstream models.

Studies on model updates examine the errors that an update introduces into a single model. Yan et al.~\cite{yan2021pctraining} show that an updated classifier can make errors on inputs that the previous classifier handled correctly, even when the updated classifier is more accurate overall. They train the updated model to reduce these errors. Shen et al.~\cite{shen2020bct} train an updated model whose feature representations remain compatible with those of the previous model. These studies consider one model and do not measure the effect of an update on downstream models.

Studies on autonomous driving pipelines examine how the errors of the perception model affect prediction. Xu et al.~\cite{xu2024realworld} show that the prediction error increases when the input comes from a real perception model instead of the ground truth. Ivanovic et al.~\cite{ivanovic2022propagating} carry the uncertainty of the perceived object positions into the prediction of object paths. Weng et al.~\cite{weng2022whose} make a prediction model robust to the errors of object tracking, which links the same object across images. These studies keep the perception model fixed. In contrast, this study updates the perception model of a pipeline in which all three modules are ML models. We then measure the change of the prediction and planning errors after each update.

\section{Methodology}
% ============================================================================

This section defines the target pipeline and the notation, and states the research questions of this study.

\subsection{Target Pipeline and Notation}
This study focuses on an autonomous driving pipeline that consists of a perception module, a prediction module and a planning module. We call this pipeline the target pipeline. We write $C_1$ for the perception model, $C_2$ for the prediction model and $C_3$ for the planning model. $C_1$ finds the objects in the image of the front camera. $C_2$ predicts the path of each object over the next 3\,s. $C_3$ chooses the path of the ego vehicle over the same 3\,s. An update is one retraining of $C_1$ on new data. We write $C_1'$ for the updated $C_1$.

We write $\delta_1$ for the gain of $C_1$ in mAP50, the standard accuracy
measure for object detection. The prediction error is the minADE, which is the
average distance between the predicted path and the true path of an object,
taken for the best of the paths that $C_2$ predicts. The nuScenes data set records the path of the ego vehicle driven by a human driver. We use this recorded path as the ground truth for planning. The planning error is the
distance between the planned position and the recorded position at
3\,s. We write $\Delta_2$ and $\Delta_3$ for the increase of the prediction
error and of the planning error after an update, respectively. An update causes entangled
enhancement when
\begin{equation}
\delta_1 > 0 \quad \text{and} \quad (\Delta_2 > 0 \ \text{or} \ \Delta_3 > 0).
\label{eq:ee}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Research Questions}
The goal of this study is to confirm whether entangled enhancement occurs in the target pipeline and to find its cause. We ask two research questions.

\noindent\textbf{RQ1:} Does an update that improves $C_1$ increase the error of $C_2$ or $C_3$? The studies in Section~II describe the risk that an update of an upstream model increases the error of the downstream models. However, none of these studies examined this risk in a real autonomous driving pipeline. To answer RQ1, we update $C_1$ and measure $\Delta_2$ and $\Delta_3$ in the pipeline.

\noindent\textbf{RQ2:} Does the increase come from the adaptation of $C_2$ and $C_3$ to the errors of $C_1$? Only training on the output of $C_1$ lets $C_2$ and $C_3$ learn the errors of $C_1$, which an update then changes. To answer RQ2, we compare $C_2$ and $C_3$ trained on the output of $C_1$ with $C_2$ and $C_3$ trained on the ground truth.

\subsection{Data Set and Its Preparation}
We use 350 of the 850 scenes of nuScenes~\cite{caesar2020nuscenes}, which are driving clips of about 20\,s from Boston and Singapore. We use the full-size images of the front camera only (4.6\,GB). A keyframe is an image with labelled objects, recorded twice per second. We split the 350 scenes into four parts. Boston~1 (100 scenes) trains $C_1$. Boston~2 (100 scenes) trains $C_2$ and $C_3$, with 14 of the scenes kept for validation. $C_1$ never sees Boston~2 during training. The output of $C_1$ on Boston~2 therefore contains the errors that $C_1$ makes on unseen data~\cite{xu2024realworld}. Singapore~1 (100 scenes) provides the new data for the updates. Singapore~2 (50 scenes, 2{,}020 images) is the test set. The test set has 1{,}720 test samples, one for every keyframe with 3\,s of future.

\subsection{Implementation of the Pipeline}
We use YOLOv11n~\cite{jocher2024yolo11} as a perception model ($C_1$), which is trained for 10 epochs on Boston~1, where an epoch is one pass over the training data. We train two initial $C_1$ with the random seeds 0 and 1, where a random seed is the starting number for the random choices made during training. The two initial $C_1$ reach an mAP50 of 0.109 and 0.117 on Singapore~2.

AutoBot-Ego~\cite{girgis2022autobots} is employed as a prediction model ($C_2$). For each object in the current keyframe, a transformer reads the objects of the last four keyframes relative to that object and outputs six possible paths of 3\,s. $C_2$ needs no hand-written rule that links objects across images~\cite{weng2022whose}.

For the planning model ($C_3$), we employ PlanT~\cite{renz2022plant}. $C_3$ receives one input for each object that $C_1$ finds. This input holds the current position of the object, the most likely path of the object predicted by $C_2$, the class of the object, and the confidence of $C_1$ in the object. Two further inputs hold the driving command and the path of the ego vehicle over the last 1.5\,s, which we call the ego path. The command is left, straight or right~\cite{zhai2023rethinking}. A recurrent decoder outputs six points of the planned path over 3\,s.

We train $C_2$ and $C_3$ on the output of $C_1$ on Boston~2, once for each initial $C_1$. We call this training mode pipe training, because the training input of $C_2$ and $C_3$ comes from $C_1$, as in the pipeline. Training stops early when the error on the validation scenes stops decreasing. The training data are augmented by mirroring and rotation. A planning module that receives the ego path can ignore the objects~\cite{li2024egostatus}. We check whether $C_3$ uses the objects by removing the objects from the input of $C_3$. The removal moves the planned position at 3\,s by 0.73 to 1.65\,m on average. This change is above the threshold of 0.5\,m, which we fixed before the experiment.

\subsection{Updates of the Perception Model}
An update retrains one initial $C_1$ on Singapore~1 with one of 15 settings. The settings differ in the amount of new data, the number of epochs and the random seed. Nine settings use 100\,\%, 50\,\% or 25\,\% of the images with 10 epochs. Six settings use all images with 5 or 20 epochs. Each of these five combinations of data and epochs is used with the seeds 1, 2 and 3. The 15 settings applied to both initial $C_1$ give 30 updates. The 30 updates are not repetitions of one procedure. The updates differ in the amount of new data, the number of epochs, the random seed and the initial $C_1$. The test scenes are never used to choose a model.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:campaign} gives the procedure for one initial $C_1$ and one training mode $r$ of $C_2$ and $C_3$. Running the procedure for both initial $C_1$ gives the 30 updates. In Algorithm~\ref{alg:campaign}, `pipe' indicates pipe training, and `iso' indicates iso training, in which $C_2$ and $C_3$ are trained on the ground truth (Section~IV-E). Line~1 trains $C_2$ and $C_3$ on Boston~2 in the training mode $r$. Line~2 measures the prediction error $E_2$ and the planning error $E_3$ of the pipeline with $C_1$ on Singapore~2. For each setting $s$, line~4 retrains $C_1$ on Singapore~1 and gives $C_1'$. Line~5 computes the gain $\delta_1$. Line~6 replaces $C_1$ with $C_1'$, keeps $C_2$ and $C_3$ fixed, and computes $\Delta_2$ and $\Delta_3$ against $E_2$ and $E_3$.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\footnotesize
\DontPrintSemicolon
\LinesNumbered
\SetKwInOut{Input}{Input}\SetKwInOut{Output}{Output}
\Input{initial $C_1$, training mode $r \in \{\mathrm{pipe}, \mathrm{iso}\}$, settings $\mathcal{S}$, Boston~2, Singapore~1, Singapore~2}
\Output{$\delta_1$, $\Delta_2$ and $\Delta_3$ for each setting}
Train $C_2^{r}, C_3^{r}$ on Boston~2 in the training mode $r$\;
$E_2, E_3 \leftarrow$ errors of $C_1 \rightarrow C_2^{r} \rightarrow C_3^{r}$ on Singapore~2\;
\ForEach{setting $s \in \mathcal{S}$}{
  $C_1' \leftarrow$ retrain $C_1$ on Singapore~1 with $s$\;
  $\delta_1 \leftarrow \mathrm{mAP50}(C_1') - \mathrm{mAP50}(C_1)$ on Singapore~2\;
  $\Delta_2, \Delta_3 \leftarrow$ errors of $C_1' \rightarrow C_2^{r} \rightarrow C_3^{r}$ on Singapore~2 minus $E_2, E_3$\;
}
\caption{Update procedure for one initial $C_1$ and one training mode}
\label{alg:campaign}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Experiment for RQ1}
To answer RQ1, we run Algorithm~\ref{alg:campaign} with pipe training for both initial $C_1$. $C_2$ and $C_3$ stay fixed during all 30 updates. The prediction error is computed over the objects found by $C_1$ that are matched one to one with a ground truth object of the same class. The outputs of $C_2$ and $C_3$ on the ground truth stay exactly the same before and after every update, which confirms that only the output of $C_1$ changes.

An increase can also come from randomness alone. The two initial $C_1$ have almost the same accuracy. Replacing one initial $C_1$ with the other still changes the errors of $C_2$ and $C_3$, although neither $C_1$ is better. We call the largest of these changes the seed variation. The seed variation is $\tau_2 = 0.112$\,m for the prediction error and $\tau_3 = 0.018$\,m for the planning error. We count the updates with an increase at three levels of evidence. The first level counts any increase. The second level counts an increase above the seed variation. The third level counts an increase whose 95\,\% bootstrap interval lies above zero. The bootstrap repeats the measurement on 2{,}000 test sets, each drawn from the 50 test scenes with replacement.

\subsection{Experiment for RQ2}
To answer RQ2, we add a second training mode. In iso training, we train $C_2$ and $C_3$ on the ground truth of Boston~2, isolated from $C_1$. Only pipe training lets $C_2$ and $C_3$ learn the errors of $C_1$. We run Algorithm~\ref{alg:campaign} with iso training for both initial $C_1$ and the same 30 updates. In each update, the iso and pipe versions of $C_2$ and $C_3$ receive the same output of $C_1'$. If the increase comes from adaptation, $\Delta_2$ and $\Delta_3$ are larger after pipe training than after iso training. We count the increases at the same three levels of evidence.

We also retrain $C_2$ and $C_3$ on the output of $C_1'$ for two further updates, one from each initial $C_1$ with all images and 10 epochs. The retraining lets $C_2$ and $C_3$ adapt to the errors of $C_1'$. If adaptation causes the increase, the retraining lowers the prediction error and the planning error.

\subsection{Results for RQ2}
In all 30 updates, $\Delta_2$ after pipe training is larger than $\Delta_2$ after iso training (red circles above blue squares in Fig.~\ref{fig:delta2}). After iso training, the prediction error increases in only 5 updates. None of these increases exceeds the seed variation (Table~\ref{tab:counts}). Both versions of $C_2$ receive the same output of $C_1'$. Only the version trained on the output of $C_1$ has learned the errors of $C_1$. Only this version increases its error beyond the seed variation. The comparison results characterize the cause of the increase of the prediction error. The increase comes from the adaptation of $C_2$ to the errors of $C_1$, which the update changes.

For the planning error, the comparison gives the opposite result. The increase appears more often after iso training (29 of 30 updates) than after pipe training (18 of 30). Adaptation cannot explain an increase after iso training. The $C_3$ trained on the ground truth receives the predicted paths of $C_2$ only at test time. A change in the objects that $C_1'$ finds can therefore affect this $C_3$ more than the $C_3$ trained on the output of $C_1$. These increases are also small, at most 0.127\,m.

The retraining of $C_2$ and $C_3$ on the output of $C_1'$ (Section~IV-E) tests the same cause from the other side. After pipe training, the two retrained updates give a prediction error of 2.75 and 2.61\,m. The planning error is 4.14 and 4.00\,m. After the retraining, the prediction error falls to 2.39 and 2.45\,m. The planning error falls to 3.71 and 3.70\,m. The retraining lowers the prediction error by 0.36 and 0.16\,m and the planning error by 0.44 and 0.30\,m. Both errors fall below the errors before the update (2.62 and 2.66\,m, 4.16 and 4.00\,m). When $C_2$ and $C_3$ adapt to the errors of $C_1'$, the update becomes an improvement of the whole pipeline. This result supports the retraining of downstream models that Wang and Machida model~\cite{wang2024compsac,wang2025issre}.

\noindent\textbf{Answer to RQ2:} For the prediction error, yes. The increase appears only when $C_2$ has learned the errors of $C_1$. The retraining on the output of $C_1'$ removes the increase. For the planning error, adaptation does not explain the small increase, because the increase appears more often after iso training.

\section{Conclusion}
% ============================================================================

In this study, we investigated the entangled enhancement in a real autonomous driving pipeline. The pipeline consists of a perception model (YOLOv11n), a prediction model (AutoBot-Ego) and a planning model (PlanT), trained on the nuScenes data set. We updated the perception model 30 times with new data. The 30 updates differ in the amount of new data, the number of training epochs, the random seed and the initial perception model.

To answer RQ1, we measured the prediction error and the planning error after each update. Entangled enhancement occurs in the prediction model in 23 of the 30 updates. In 11 of these updates, the increase of the prediction error is larger than the seed variation. To answer RQ2, we compared the prediction and planning models trained on the output of the perception model with the models trained on the ground truth. An increase of the prediction error above the seed variation appears only after training on the output of the perception model. Retraining the prediction and planning models on the output of the updated perception model lowers both errors below their values before the update. These results indicate that the adaptation of the prediction model to the errors of the perception model causes entangled enhancement. As future work, we develop a method that detects entangled enhancement after an update of the perception model, before ground truth for the new data exists.
