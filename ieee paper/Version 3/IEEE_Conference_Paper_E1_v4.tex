\begin{abstract}
Autonomous Vehicles (AVs) are increasingly built as a pipeline of separately
trained perception, prediction, and planning models. Because the models are
trained separately, each one is usually updated on its own while the others stay
fixed. Retraining a single upstream model can nonetheless degrade the models
downstream of it, because those models were built and evaluated on the output of
the earlier version. This failure is known as entangled enhancement, where an
upstream model improves on its own metric while the system as a whole degrades.
It has so far been studied analytically in two-model systems, and has not been
investigated across an AV pipeline. In this paper, we first formally define
entangled enhancement in the perception--prediction--planning pipeline,
separating a model's own error from the error it inherits from upstream by
measuring every downstream model both on ground-truth inputs and on the live
output of its neighbour. We then measure it on a three-model pipeline built from
the nuScenes dataset, retraining the perception model across a geographic domain
shift and recording the effect on the prediction and planning models. Across $15$
retraining updates that vary the fine-tuning data size, the number of epochs, and
the random seed, the perception model improves every time, and in $6$ of the
updates the L2 displacement error of the planning model rises at the same time.
Repeating the campaign on a class-balanced training split gives the same outcome
in $4$ of a further $12$ updates. The two planning metrics also diverge. On one
update the collision rate falls to zero while L2 agreement worsens, so no single
metric on the system output is sufficient to admit an update.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================================
Autonomous Vehicles (AVs) are increasingly built as a pipeline of separately trained
perception, prediction, and planning models. Perception detects the surrounding agents,
prediction forecasts their motion, and planning selects the driving trajectory~\cite{chen2024e2esurvey,hu2023uniad}. Because the models are trained separately, each one can be replaced without retraining the others. In deployment, such a pipeline is maintained under domain drift one model at a time. A perception model is
retrained for a new city, a prediction model is updated when traffic changes, and a planning model is revised for new regulations.

\looseness=-1
In practice, one model is usually retrained while the others stay fixed. The retrained model then becomes better on its own metric, while the models after it become worse. The reason is that those models were built and evaluated on the output of the earlier version of the retrained model. The first reported
instance of this self-defeating behavior appeared in an object-detection setting
\cite{wu2021fixesthatfail}, where a better perception model reduced the
accuracy of the downstream task. Wang and Machida~\cite{wang2024compsac} modeled and analyzed entangled enhancement in a two-model machine learning system by a
continuous-time Markov chain. Entangled enhancement has also been reported in a
sentence-classification system~\cite{wang2025issre}. Sculley et al.~\cite{sculley2015hidden} describe the same difficulty more generally. When learning components depend on each other, changing one of them creates maintenance problems for the others. However, no existing study examines entangled
enhancement across an entire perception--prediction--planning pipeline for
AVs. Although previous studies also consider the perception module of an AV, their analysis was limited to two sequential models and focused only on the perception component.

\looseness=-1
This failure mode is specific to pipelines of separately trained models. An end-to-end network is trained as a single model, so its components are not updated independently and no interface exists between them to shift. To address this gap, this paper studies how a single-model retraining event
propagates through an AV pipeline, using empirical analysis. For the empirical analysis, we build a three-model pipeline on the nuScenes
dataset~\cite{caesar2020nuscenes}. The perception model is
YOLOv11n~\cite{jocher2024yolo11}, an object detection model applied to the front-camera
image. The prediction model is a constant-velocity trajectory model. The
planning model is a rule-based planner from the Intelligent Driver Model (IDM)
family~\cite{treiber2000idm}, whose proposal-and-score design follows
PDM-Closed~\cite{dauner2023parting}. We train the perception model on Boston
scenes and fine-tune it on Singapore scenes, a well-known domain gap within
nuScenes, and investigate how the updates affect the downstream performance. To separate a
model's own error from the error it inherits from upstream, each downstream model
is measured in two modes, one with ground-truth inputs and one with the live
upstream output. Because a single retraining is only one data point, we run a
campaign of $15$ retraining updates that vary the amount of fine-tuning data, the
number of training epochs, and the random seed.

\vspace{2pt}
This paper makes two contributions.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
\item We formally define entangled enhancement in the
perception--prediction--planning pipeline for AVs, and introduce the cascade coupling factor that quantifies the intensity of entangled enhancement.

\item We empirically investigate entangled enhancement in an AV pipeline built from the nuScenes dataset, showing that six out of $15$ retraining updates exhibit it.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:method} formalizes the pipeline model and the definition of
entangled enhancement.
Section~\ref{sec:setup} details the experimental setup and the retraining
campaign. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the empirical evidence of entangled
enhancement.
Section~\ref{sec:implications} discusses the mechanism and its implications,
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{System Model and Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Formal Pipeline Model}
We model an AV pipeline as a composition of three functions,
\begin{equation}
S = C_1 \circ C_2 \circ C_3,
\qquad
S(x) = C_3\bigl(C_2(C_1(x))\bigr),
\label{eq:comp}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is an input camera frame processed through the three models:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Perception ($C_1$).} The output $z_1 = C_1(x)$ is a list of the objects the model found in the image. For each object it gives the type, the size, and the position on the road relative to the ego vehicle.
\item \textbf{Prediction ($C_2$).} The output $z_2 = C_2(z_1)$ is, for every
detected object, a forecast trajectory over the next several seconds.
\item \textbf{Planning ($C_3$).} The output $z_3 = C_3(z_2)$ is the ego vehicle's
planned trajectory, a sequence of positions over a $3$\,s horizon.
\end{itemize}
The outputs $z_1$ and $z_2$ connect one model to the next. A change in $C_1$
changes $z_1$, which is the input of $C_2$. The change then reaches
$z_2$, which is the input of $C_3$. Now suppose that only $C_1$ is retrained. The
parameters of $C_2$ and $C_3$ are unchanged. Any change in their outputs
therefore comes only from their inputs.

\subsection{Entangled Enhancement}
\label{sec:ee-def}
Entangled enhancement occurs when an upstream model is retrained and improves its own performance, and yet the system as a whole degrades~\cite{wang2024compsac}.
This entanglement happens because the downstream models were tuned to the
statistics of the \emph{original} upstream output and are shifted after the upstream model update. We denote by $\delta_1$ the improvement of the perception performance of
$C_1$ after retraining, and by $\Delta_3$ the
degradation of the planning performance of $C_3$. Entangled enhancement is formally specified by the joint condition
\begin{equation}
\underbrace{\delta_1 > 0}_{C_1\ \text{improves}}
\ \wedge\
\underbrace{\Delta_3 > 0}_{\text{system degrades}}.
\label{eq:ee}
\end{equation}
The condition requires the upstream model to improve on its \emph{own} performance
($\delta_1 > 0$) while the system nonetheless degrades ($\Delta_3 > 0$). The upstream model must improve for this to be entangled enhancement. If the upstream model became worse and the system became worse with it, that is an ordinary regression.

\subsection{Two-Mode Measurement and the Isolation Property}
\label{sec:dualmode}
To make entangled enhancement measurable, we separate a model's own error from
the error inherited from the upstream models. Let $z_k$ denote the output of model $C_k$,
and let $M_k(z_k)$ be an error metric evaluated on that output, with lower values
better. We evaluate each downstream model in two modes. In \emph{isolated} mode a
model receives ground-truth inputs, and its output is written $z_k^{\text{iso}}$.
For $C_2$ and $C_3$,
\begin{align}
z_2^{\text{iso}} &= C_2(z_1^{\star}), &
z_3^{\text{iso}} &= C_3(z_2^{\star}),
\label{eq:iso-mode}
\end{align}
where $z_k^{\star}$ is the ground-truth value of $C_k$. In \emph{pipeline}
mode a model receives the direct output of the upstream neighbor, and its output is
written $z_k^{\text{pipe}}$,
\begin{align}
z_2^{\text{pipe}} &= C_2(C_1(x)), &
z_3^{\text{pipe}} &= C_3(C_2(C_1(x))).
\label{eq:pipe-mode}
\end{align}
The metric in isolated mode, $M_k(z_k^{\text{iso}})$, reflects the model's own
error, while the metric in pipeline mode, $M_k(z_k^{\text{pipe}})$, reflects its
own error together with the error inherited from the upstream model. The
difference between the two is the inherited part. Isolated mode needs
ground-truth inputs, so it can be run only in an experiment, not in a real
vehicle. It shows whether a change came from the model itself or from its input.

Consider a retraining event that replaces an upstream model $C_k$ by an updated
model $C_k'$, whose outputs we write $z_k'$. If the pipeline is properly composed,
the retraining leaves the isolated-mode output of every downstream model that was
not retrained unchanged, because that output depends only on the ground-truth
inputs. We call
this the \emph{isolation property},
\begin{equation}
M_k(z_k^{\text{iso}}) = M_k(z_k^{\text{iso}\,\prime}).
\label{eq:iso}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:iso} is a testable property of the setup rather than an
assumption. Any change in the isolated-mode metric of a model that was not retrained indicates
contamination, and we verify the equation in Section~\ref{sec:results}. The effect
that the retraining causes at model $C_k$ is the change in its pipeline
metric,
\begin{equation}
\Delta_k = M_k(z_k^{\text{pipe}\,\prime}) - M_k(z_k^{\text{pipe}}),
\label{eq:inj}
\end{equation}
which, given~\eqref{eq:iso}, is entirely inherited from the upstream update.

\subsection{Coupling Factor}
\label{sec:diag}
To quantify the intensity of entangled enhancement, we relate the system performance degradation $\Delta_3$ to the upstream
improvement $\delta_1$, through the \emph{cascade coupling
factor}
\begin{equation}
\rho_{1\to 3} = \frac{\Delta_3}{\delta_1}.
\label{eq:rho-def}
\end{equation}
Under the sign convention above, a positive $\delta_1$ improves the upstream model
and a positive $\Delta_3$ worsens the system. Entangled enhancement therefore
corresponds to $\rho_{1\to 3} > 0$, and the magnitude of $\rho_{1\to 3}$ measures
how much regression of the planning metric accompanies a unit of upstream
improvement. The factor is defined whenever $\delta_1 \neq 0$, which holds for
every update reported in this paper, and it can be computed from the $\delta_1$
and $\Delta_3$ columns of Table~\ref{tab:campaign}.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
% ============================================================================

The objective of this section is to construct a pipeline in which a single
retraining event can be applied to $C_1$ and its downstream effect measured under
exact controls. We describe the dataset and the three models, the retraining
procedure, the metrics recorded at each model, and the campaign of $15$ updates
over which they are measured.

\subsection{Dataset and Components}
We use the full nuScenes v1.0 trainval split~\cite{caesar2020nuscenes}. A
nuScenes \emph{scene} is a $\approx 20$\,s driving clip, annotated at $2$\,Hz. Each
of these annotated samples is a \emph{keyframe}. A scene contributes
about $40$ keyframes, and we use the front-camera (\texttt{CAM\_FRONT}) keyframe
as one evaluation frame throughout. The trainval split contains $850$ scenes
($34{,}149$ keyframes in total, $\approx 45$\,GB), of which $467$ scenes were
recorded in Boston and $383$ in Singapore. Partitioning by recorded location and
splitting each city $60/40$ into train and validation gives
\texttt{boston\_train}/\texttt{boston\_val} ($280/187$ scenes) and
\texttt{singapore\_train}/\texttt{singapore\_val} ($229/154$ scenes). The Boston
perception model is trained on the $11{,}087$ \texttt{CAM\_FRONT} keyframes of
\texttt{boston\_train}. All measurements use a fixed subset of \texttt{singapore\_val}, obtained by
taking every third scene of its $154$ scenes to give $50$ scenes, comprising
$n=2{,}020$ annotated keyframes. The subset keeps the evaluation affordable
across the $15$ fine-tunes of the campaign while still spanning the whole city. This same
$50$-scene subset is reused, unchanged, for every one of the $15$ updates, which
keeps the comparisons exact. For $C_1$ training, the 3D box annotations are
projected onto the front-camera keyframes to produce 2D labels over the eight
driving classes that the pipeline consumes, namely car, truck, bus, trailer,
construction vehicle, motorcycle, bicycle, and pedestrian.

The perception model $C_1$ is YOLOv11n~\cite{jocher2024yolo11}, an object
detection model applied to the front-camera image. We fine-tune it for $10$ epochs on
\texttt{boston\_train} from COCO weights to obtain the original model. To generate
an updated model, the same YOLOv11n is fine-tuned further on
\texttt{singapore\_train} starting from the Boston checkpoint. At inference, each
2D detection is lifted to an ego-frame ground position by intersecting its
bottom-center pixel ray with the ground plane.

The prediction model $C_2$ is a constant-velocity motion model, which is
deterministic and carries no trainable parameters. It forecasts an agent by
projecting it forward at whatever velocity it is given. In isolated mode it is
given the ground-truth velocity of each agent. In pipeline mode it receives the
detections of $C_1$ on a single frame, which carry position but no velocity, so
the projection is made at zero velocity and the forecast holds each agent at its
detected position. Any change in its pipeline output is therefore attributable
solely to a change in $C_1$.

The planning model $C_3$ is a rule-based planner of the proposal-and-score type,
built on the Intelligent Driver Model~\cite{treiber2000idm}. It works in two
stages. In the proposal stage it enumerates a finite set $\mathcal{P}$ of
candidate trajectories, obtained by sweeping target speeds and lateral offsets
around the current lane. In the scoring stage it selects the candidate
$\pi^{\star}$ of minimum cost,
\begin{equation}
\pi^{\star} = \arg\min_{\pi \in \mathcal{P}}\
J_{\text{prog}}(\pi)
\ +\ \lambda \!\!\sum_{a \in z_2} \phi\bigl(d(\pi, a)\bigr).
\label{eq:idm}
\end{equation}
The cost has two terms. The progress term $J_{\text{prog}}(\pi)$ penalises a
candidate for advancing slowly along the route. The safety term penalises a
candidate for passing close to any predicted agent. For an agent $a$ in the
predicted futures $z_2$, $d(\pi,a)$ is the closest distance between candidate
$\pi$ and that agent over the next $3$ seconds, and the penalty $\phi$ is a
decreasing function of this distance. A candidate that passes near an agent
therefore receives a large penalty. The weight $\lambda$ sets how strongly safety
is traded against progress.
This design reproduces the PDM-Closed~\cite{dauner2023parting} architecture without
the learned scorer. The safety term is the only place where $C_3$ reads
$z_2$. A detection that moves, appears, or disappears changes the predicted
futures, which changes the safety term, which changes the selected candidate.
This is the path by which a retraining of $C_1$ reaches the plan.

\subsection{Retraining Procedure}
\label{sec:experiment}
The source domain is Boston and the target domain is Singapore. The shift between
them is a well-studied domain gap within nuScenes, with left-hand versus
right-hand traffic, different vehicle and agent mixes, and different lighting
conditions~\cite{caesar2020nuscenes}. Before the experiments begin, $C_1$ is
trained on the source domain to obtain the original $C_1$, and $C_2$ and $C_3$
are frozen for the remainder of the study.

Each retraining event consists of three steps, in which $C_1$ is the only model
that changes.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The original pipeline is measured on \texttt{singapore\_val}, recording the
detection quality of $C_1$ together with the metrics of $C_2$ and $C_3$ in
isolated and pipeline mode.
\item $C_1$ is fine-tuned on the target domain to obtain the updated model
$C_1'$.
\item The updated pipeline is measured on the same frames. Comparing the two
records verifies the isolation property~\eqref{eq:iso} and gives the
changes~\eqref{eq:inj} at $C_2$ and $C_3$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
The perception model $C_1$ is scored with standard detection metrics (mAP@50,
mAP@50-95, precision, recall) on the target-domain validation images.

The prediction model $C_2$ is scored with the minimum average displacement error
(minADE), the standard metric for predictors that may output several candidate
futures, or modes, for one agent. For an agent with forecast positions
$\hat{p}^{(m)}_{t}$ under mode $m$ and ground-truth positions $p^{\star}_{t}$
over $T$ future steps,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{minADE} = \min_{m}\ \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\bigl\lVert \hat{p}^{(m)}_{t} - p^{\star}_{t} \bigr\rVert_2 ,
\label{eq:minade}
\end{equation}
averaged over agents. The prediction model used here is deterministic and has a single
mode, so the minimum is taken over that one mode.

The planning model $C_3$ is scored with the L2 displacement error between the
planned ego trajectory $\hat{e}_t$ and the trajectory the human driver actually
took, $e^{\star}_t$, measured $\tau$ seconds ahead of the current frame,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{L2}@\tau = \bigl\lVert \hat{e}_{\tau} - e^{\star}_{\tau} \bigr\rVert_2 ,
\qquad \tau \in \{1,2,3\}\ \text{s}.
\label{eq:l2}
\end{equation}
The collision rate is reported alongside it.

The \emph{plan shift} is recorded for the campaign. It is the distance between
the plan produced with the original $C_1$ and the plan produced with the updated
$C_1'$ on the same scene. It measures how far a retraining moves the driving
decision, without reference to the human path.

L2 is the standard open-loop planning protocol of nuScenes, and earlier studies
on end-to-end driving models use it as well~\cite{hu2023uniad,jiang2023vad,zhai2023rethinking}.
The protocol has known limitations. We use it differentially, comparing two runs
that differ only in $C_1$, and Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:related}
discuss what this comparison does and does not show.

\subsection{The Retraining Campaign}
\label{sec:campaign}
We run a campaign of \textbf{$15$ retraining updates} of $C_1$, each measured
through the three steps of Section~\ref{sec:experiment}. The campaign varies three factors.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Fine-tuning data size.} We fine-tune $C_1$ on three amounts of
Singapore data, namely the full \texttt{singapore\_train} set of $229$ scenes,
one half of it, and one quarter of it. The half and the quarter are obtained by
taking every second and every fourth scene of a fixed scene ordering, which makes
the same subsets available to every run. A smaller set gives $C_1$ less data
for adapting to the new city. Varying this amount therefore tests whether the
downstream effect depends on how much $C_1$ was able to adapt.
\item \textbf{Training length.} We use $5$, $10$, and $20$ epochs, to test whether
longer fine-tuning changes the downstream effect.
\item \textbf{Random seed.} We repeat each setting with three seeds, which
separates a result from seed noise.
\end{itemize}
These three factors are combined into three data sizes at $10$ epochs, plus two
additional training lengths ($5$ and $20$) at the full data size, each repeated
over three seeds, giving $3\times3 + 2\times3 = 15$ updates. Only the Singapore fine-tuned $C_1'$ differs between the updates. The
Boston-trained $C_1$ and the frozen $C_2$ and $C_3$ are shared across all $15$.
Within each update the fine-tuning run uses a single fixed random seed. The
evaluation of $C_2$ and $C_3$ is deterministic, because neither model has
trainable parameters. Repeating a
measurement reproduces the same numbers exactly. The isolation
property~\eqref{eq:iso} can be verified by checking that the isolated-mode
metrics are identical in the two runs, and any difference at all, however small,
indicates a fault in the measurement.

\subsection{Measurement Protocol}
For each of the $15$ updates we record the metrics of Section~\ref{sec:metrics} on
\texttt{singapore\_val}, once with $C_1$ in place and once with $C_1'$ in place.
From the two records we compute $\delta_1$, $\Delta_2$, $\Delta_3$, the plan shift,
and whether the update meets the condition for entangled enhancement
(Section~\ref{sec:ee-def}).

% ============================================================================
\section{Evaluation Results}
\label{sec:results}
% ============================================================================
This section reports whether entangled enhancement occurs in the AV pipeline.

We begin with a single retraining event and ask whether entangled enhancement
occurs in it (Section~\ref{sec:res-ref}). A positive answer raises two doubts
that must be settled before the result can be believed. The planning model may have
degraded because the retrained $C_1$ was worse, which would make the case an
ordinary regression, so we check the perception model
(Section~\ref{sec:res-decouple}). The change may also come from the measurement
setup rather than from the update, so we check that the models we did not retrain
were untouched (Section~\ref{sec:res-iso}). With both doubts settled, we locate
the point along the pipeline where the retraining has its largest effect
(Section~\ref{sec:res-interface}).

A single event cannot show how often the condition arises. We therefore repeat
the measurement over the $15$ updates of the campaign
(Section~\ref{sec:res-campaign}). One explanation remains available even then,
namely that the effect is an artifact of the class imbalance in the training
data, and the final experiment removes that imbalance and repeats the campaign
over a further $12$ updates (Section~\ref{sec:res-balanced}).
Section~\ref{sec:res-diag} reports the training diagnostics that the preceding
results rely on.

\subsection{The Reference Update}
\label{sec:res-ref}
A single retraining event is enough to test whether the joint
condition~\eqref{eq:ee} can be met in practice. The reference update is the
full-data, $10$-epoch, seed-1 fine-tune, and Table~\ref{tab:results} reports its
measurements on \texttt{singapore\_val} ($n=2{,}020$ frames).

The perception model improves on its own metric, with $\delta_1 = +0.119$ as mAP@50 rises
from $0.137$ to $0.256$. The input it gives to $C_2$ improves as well, by
$\Delta_2 = -2.17$\,m, a $27\%$ reduction. $C_3$ nonetheless degrades. Its
L2 error against the human trajectory rises from $5.55$ to $5.67$\,m, so
$\Delta_3 = +0.124$\,m. Both clauses of~\eqref{eq:ee} are satisfied, and
the update is an instance of entangled enhancement in an AV pipeline. Sculley et
al.~\cite{sculley2015hidden} describe this kind of failure qualitatively; the
measurements here quantify one case of it under controlled conditions. The
degradation originates in the input rather than in $C_3$ itself. In
isolated mode the L2 error of $C_3$ is $4.64$\,m in both runs, because the
planning model receives identical ground-truth inputs. Only the pipeline-mode value
moves, and Fig.~\ref{fig:cascade} shows the two pairs.

The two metrics of $C_3$ move in opposite directions. The collision rate falls
from $0.020$ to zero over the same update in which the L2 error rises. A
single-metric check on the system output would have reached opposite
verdicts depending on which metric it read.

\subsection{Perception Quality After Retraining}
\label{sec:res-decouple}
If the retrained $C_1$ were weaker than the original, the planning degradation
would be an ordinary regression rather than entangled enhancement. The
measurements rule this out. The mAP@50 of $C_1$ rises by $86.6\%$, and the
$229$-scene Singapore fine-tune set is large enough that $C_1$ adapts to
the new city rather than over-fitting it. The planning degradation therefore
cannot be attributed to a weaker perception model. $C_1$ is not uniformly
strong, however. The confusion matrix of the
Boston-trained $C_1$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion}) shows the dominant \texttt{car}
class well separated, while the rarer classes are still frequently confused with
background. Section~\ref{sec:res-balanced} examines whether this residual class
imbalance is what produces the effect.

\subsection{The Isolation Control}
\label{sec:res-iso}
The measured change can be attributed to the update only if $C_2$ and $C_3$ were
themselves untouched by it. Because both models are deterministic and have no
trainable parameters, feeding them identical ground-truth inputs must return
identical values. Any difference would mean that state or data had leaked between
the two runs.

The isolated-mode rows of Table~\ref{tab:results} show no such difference. The
metric of $C_2$ is $1.1211$\,m in both runs, and the metric of $C_3$ is
$0.7458$, $2.4100$ and $4.6384$\,m at $1$, $2$ and $3$ seconds in both runs, to
every recorded digit. The same equality held for all $15$ updates of the
campaign. The pipeline-mode changes in Table~\ref{tab:results} can therefore be
attributed to the retraining of $C_1$, reaching $C_2$ and $C_3$ through their
inputs.

\subsection{Location of the Largest Effect}
\label{sec:res-interface}
\looseness=-1
Knowing where the retraining has its largest effect indicates where a maintenance
check should be placed. In Table~\ref{tab:results} that effect is at the step
from $C_1$ to $C_2$. Under the Boston-trained $C_1$, the metric of $C_2$ in
pipeline mode was $7.9310$\,m against $1.1211$\,m in isolated mode, a gap of
$6.81$\,m. After the Singapore fine-tune the pipeline-mode value fell to
$5.7590$\,m, closing the gap to $4.64$\,m. This improvement of $27.39\%$ was
obtained without modifying $C_2$ at all.

The gap itself has two sources, because $C_2$ uses ground-truth velocity in
isolated mode and a zero-velocity rollout in pipeline mode
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}). It measures the detection error of $C_1$
together with the loss of velocity information, rather than the detection error
alone. The velocity term is the same in both runs, so the change
$\Delta_2 = -2.17$\,m between them is still attributable to $C_1$ alone.

\subsection{Campaign Results}
\label{sec:res-campaign}
One update cannot show how often the condition arises, or how strongly, when the
retraining configuration is varied. Table~\ref{tab:campaign} reports all $15$
updates of the campaign (Section~\ref{sec:campaign}).

Fine-tuning on the new city improves the perception model every time. All $15$ updates
have $\delta_1 > 0$, and all $15$ improve the prediction model as well, with
$\Delta_2 < 0$. The planning model nonetheless degrades in $6$ of those $15$ updates,
which meets the condition for entangled enhancement. The degradations range from $+0.008$\,m, which is small
enough that it may not exceed the run-to-run variation of the measurement, to $+0.193$\,m. Reaching
this condition required no intervention on the training data.

The remaining $9$ updates improve the planning model. Nothing in the training
configuration separates the two groups, so an update that improves $C_1$
cannot be assumed to improve the plan, and the effect has to be measured at the
downstream models rather than predicted from $C_1$.

\subsection{Robustness to Class Imbalance}
\label{sec:res-balanced}
The campaign reaches the condition without any intervention on the training data.
The effect could still be an artifact of the residual class imbalance visible in
Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion}, where rare classes are confused with background.

We therefore repeat the campaign on a class-balanced training split. Images
containing rare classes are oversampled so that the per-class instance
distribution flattens, using a repeat factor $(n_{\max}/n_c)^{p}$ per class $c$
clipped to $[1,12]$. The validation split is left untouched, so the metrics
remain comparable with those of the first campaign. This second campaign varies
two balance strengths ($p \in \{0.5, 1.0\}$), two training lengths ($10$ and $20$
epochs), and three seeds, giving a further $12$ updates.

Balancing the classes does not remove the effect. The perception model improves in all
$12$ updates, and the planning model degrades in $4$ of them. Three of those four occur
at $p = 0.5$, with $\delta_1$ up to $+0.143$ and $\Delta_3$ up to $+0.139$\,m,
and one at $p = 1.0$, with $\delta_1 = +0.125$ and $\Delta_3 = +0.075$\,m. Cases
appear at both balance strengths and at both training lengths. The residual class
imbalance is therefore not the source of the effect.

\subsection{Training Diagnostics}
\label{sec:res-diag}
The results above assume that each fine-tune actually converged, and that the
$C_1$ was not left under-trained or over-fitted. The training curves confirm
this. Both the Boston and the Singapore fine-tunes converged within their epoch
budgets, and Fig.~\ref{fig:curves} shows the Boston run. The box, classification
and distribution-focal losses decrease on both the training and the validation
split, while precision, recall and mAP@50 increase, with mAP@50 rising from
$0.116$ at epoch 1 to $0.137$ at epoch 10.

The cost of one update is what makes a campaign of this size possible. A single
update, comprising one fine-tune and the two evaluations, takes about $24$
GPU-minutes, so the $15$-update campaign ran in about six GPU-hours. Training an
end-to-end planner requires about $144$ GPU-hours~\cite{sun2024sparsedrive}, at
which cost a campaign of repeated retraining is not practical.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:implications}
% ============================================================================
The measurements show that an improved perception model can degrade the plan, but not why.
This section explains the mechanism and draws its implications for maintenance.

\subsection{Mechanism of the Cascade}
Under the Boston-trained $C_1$, the forecasts reaching $C_3$ carried
$6.81$\,m more error than the same model produces on ground-truth inputs. At that
size of error, agents were missed altogether or placed far from their true
positions, so the safety term of~\eqref{eq:idm} was rarely triggered. $C_3$ then reverted to its own speed and lane rules, and on
these scenes those rules happened to produce trajectories close to the one the
human driver took.

The updated $C_1$ placed detections near their true positions, which activated
the safety term. $C_3$ began to react to agents it had previously not seen,
and its trajectories moved away from the human path. $C_3$ had not changed;
what changed was how often its safety term was active. Two consequences follow. A
downstream model can absorb upstream error, so its measured quality understates
how much it depends on the upstream input. An upstream improvement can then
expose that dependence. An updated model therefore cannot be admitted on the
basis of its own output alone.

\looseness=-1
The same mechanism cautions against reading the planning metric too literally.
The collision rate fell from $0.020$ to zero over the reference update, while L2
rose. The higher L2 arises because the improved $C_1$ caused $C_3$ to
react to real agents instead of following speed and lane rules that happened to
track the recorded human path. This is a known weakness of open-loop L2 on
nuScenes: prediction that ignores perception entirely remains
competitive~\cite{zhai2023rethinking}, and open-loop error is weakly tied to
closed-loop driving quality~\cite{codevilla2018offline}. A worse score on this
metric can accompany a safer planning model. The planning results here should
be read as reduced agreement with the human trajectory rather than as an
established safety regression, and a closed-loop or NAVSIM-style
metric~\cite{dauner2024navsim} would be the appropriate check.

\subsection{Implications for Maintenance}
The results give three rules for admitting an update. First, an update must not
be admitted on the updated model's own metric, because every update in the
campaign improved that metric. Second, admission must not stop at the next model
either, because the entangled-enhancement cases are exactly those in which $C_1$
improved and $C_2$ improved while $C_3$ degraded. Third, a maintenance policy
should assess several candidate updates rather than a single one, because the
effect on planning varies across updates. Identifying the condition needs both
ends of the pipeline. A change in the
planning metric alone does not identify it, because $\Delta_3 > 0$ also describes
an ordinary regression in which $C_1$ became worse, so the perception metric
$\delta_1$ has to be read alongside it.

A cheaper check at the upstream interface does not substitute for the downstream
measurement. To test this, we compared the detections of the updated $C_1$
against those of the original $C_1$ on the same scenes, for each of the $15$
updates, using two-sample tests on the per-image count, the confidence, the
box-center position, and the class histogram. The resulting score tracked how far
the plan moved, with a rank correlation of $\approx 0.56$, but it did not
separate the updates that worsened the planning metric from the rest. Its area
under the receiver operating characteristic curve was $\approx 0.48$, where
$0.5$ is the value expected from random ordering. Every retrained $C_1$
improved, so the scores fall in
a narrow range in which the two groups overlap. How much the output of $C_1$
moves therefore indicates the size of the downstream disturbance but not its
direction, and the effect at $C_3$ has to be measured rather than predicted from
upstream.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{End-to-End Driving and Maintenance Limits}
UniAD put detection, tracking, mapping, motion forecasting, occupancy
prediction, and planning into a single planning-oriented
network~\cite{hu2023uniad}. VAD replaced dense rasterized scene
representations with vectorized ones for efficiency~\cite{jiang2023vad}.
SparseDrive introduced fully sparse scene representations and reported the
resource profile of the flagship systems: $7.2\times$ faster training and
$5\times$ faster inference than UniAD, whose training takes about 144
GPU-hours~\cite{sun2024sparsedrive}. The main survey of the field lists
interpretability, causal confusion, and robustness under distribution shift
as open challenges, and it does not treat component-level maintenance or
retraining policy at all~\cite{chen2024e2esurvey}.

A recent end-to-end repair literature addresses maintenance directly. CorrectAD
builds a self-correction loop that diagnoses failures, generates targeted
training video with a diffusion model, and fine-tunes the entire end-to-end planner
on old plus new data, correcting 62.5\% of failure cases on
nuScenes~\cite{ma2025correctad}. R2SE avoids full-model retraining by freezing
the generalist model and training reinforcement-learned low-rank adapter
specialists on failure regions, motivated by the catastrophic forgetting that
naive fine-tuning causes~\cite{liu2025r2se}. Both reintroduce pipeline structure
to make maintenance tractable, which motivates the pipeline architecture adopted
in this work.

\subsection{Evaluation of Driving Systems}
Our planning metric is open-loop displacement error on nuScenes, and the
limits of this protocol are well documented. Codevilla et al. showed as early
as 2018 that offline prediction error correlates only weakly with actual
closed-loop driving quality~\cite{codevilla2018offline}. Zhai et al. found
that a trivial MLP using only ego state, with no perception at all, matches
perception-based end-to-end methods on nuScenes open-loop
L2~\cite{zhai2023rethinking}. Dauner et al. showed that open-loop
ego-forecasting and closed-loop driving are misaligned tasks, and that a
largely rule-based planner, PDM-Closed, outperformed learned
planners~\cite{dauner2023parting}. Their later NAVSIM benchmark provides
short-horizon non-reactive metrics that align far better with closed-loop
performance~\cite{dauner2024navsim}. These critiques target open-loop metrics
used to rank architectures. Our use is differential, comparing two runs with the
same planning model, the same scenes and one upstream change, so a bias common to both
runs cancels. We report the collision rate alongside L2 and return to the caveat
in Section~\ref{sec:implications}.

\subsection{Model Updates, Regression, and Interface Compatibility}
What we measure at system scale has per-sample analogues in the model-update
literature. Yan et al. showed that model updates cause negative flips, samples
the old model handled correctly that the new model gets wrong, at rates of
4--5\% even between equal-accuracy retrains of identical architectures, and
they proposed positive-congruent training with focal distillation to suppress
them~\cite{yan2021pctraining}. The backward-compatible training of Shen et al.
constrains a new upstream representation model so that unchanged downstream
consumers keep working~\cite{shen2020bct}, which is the same situation as
updating $C_1$ beneath an unchanged $C_2$ and $C_3$. On the monitoring side, the data
validation system of Breck et al. runs two-sample distribution tests between
pipeline stages in production~\cite{breck2019data}. On a nuScenes
detector--tracker--predictor stack of a similar structure to ours, Ivanovic et
al. showed that propagating state uncertainty across the
perception--prediction interface, instead of passing point estimates, improves
forecasting accuracy~\cite{ivanovic2022propagating}. These works
provide mitigation techniques. This paper contributes the complementary
controlled measurement of the cascade that such techniques are intended to
mitigate.

\subsection{Reliability Modeling of Machine Learning Systems}
The technical-debt analysis of Sculley et al. introduced the principle that
changing anything changes everything, together with unstable data
dependencies, and correction cascades as qualitative failure modes of ML
systems~\cite{sculley2015hidden}. The base paper for this work, Wang and
Machida~\cite{wang2024compsac}, formalized imperfect retraining of a
two-model machine learning system as a continuous-time Markov chain, named entangled
enhancement, and compared progressive and conservative maintenance policies.
Their follow-on work casts the same problem as an availability--continuity
trade-off~\cite{wang2025issre}. Both are analytical models whose state space
grows as $2^N$ for $N$ components, and both are calibrated on synthetic
transition rates. This paper provides an empirical counterpart. The isolation
property we verify empirically is the property their state factorization assumes, and
the coupling factor we propose (Section~\ref{sec:diag}) is a measured statistic
that can replace those synthetic rates.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================================
We presented a controlled study of single-model retraining in a
perception--prediction--planning pipeline under a geographic domain shift. The
two-mode measurement, with a verified isolation control, was applied on the full
nuScenes trainval split ($2{,}020$ evaluation frames) across a campaign of $15$
retraining updates rather than a single event.

Retraining $C_1$ disturbed the downstream plan in every case. The perception model
improved on its own metric in all $15$ updates, and in $6$ of them the L2 error
of the planning model rose at the same time. Repeating the campaign on a class-balanced
training split gave the same outcome in $4$ of a further $12$ updates, so the
effect does not depend on the class imbalance of the original data. Entangled
enhancement has so far been studied analytically, and these measurements show
that it is also observable in an AV pipeline.

The two planning metrics moreover diverge. On the reference update the collision
rate improved to zero while L2 agreement worsened. Each of the six cases would
have passed a review of $C_1$ alone, and also a review of the collision
rate alone. Neither model-level validation nor any single-metric check on the
system output is sufficient for safe maintenance. Measuring a cascade under exact
controls requires an architecture that exposes the connections between its
models, which is what the pipeline setting provides.
